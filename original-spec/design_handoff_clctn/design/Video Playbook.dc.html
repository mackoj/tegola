Short answer to "should we start with ads": no, not yet — and not because ads are bad, but because you don't yet know your conversion rate, and paid media is a machine for multiplying a number you haven't measured. Organic first. Then, if a clip works organically, put money behind that exact clip and only then.

You'd be buying euros for five euros. That maths only inverts under three conditions, and none are true today: a subscription with a long tail, a marketplace take rate, or a creative so good its organic reach subsidises the paid spend. The third is achievable — which is what the rest of this page is for.

One more thing in your favour: this app is a visual product in a category built entirely on people showing off their shelves. The content practically exists already. Your competitors' problem is that a database is unfilmable. Yours turns.
01 · The three platforms
Different jobs,
not different sizes
TIKTOK — DISCOVERY
Raw, first-person, no branding
The only platform where a nobody can reach 200k people with one clip. Rules: shot on a phone, face or hands in frame, no logo in the first three seconds, spoken hook, text on screen because most watch muted. Anything that looks like an ad dies in the first second. Hobby hashtags matter more than reach hashtags — #vinylcollection, not #app.
INSTAGRAM — CREDIBILITY
Beautiful, and where the collectors already live
Every serious collector community has an Instagram side. Reels for the turning sleeve and the shelf scroll; carousels for "the 11 fields that describe a graded comic" — the same schema posts that work on forums perform here as saveable slides. This is also where you DM creators, and where a shop or a recyclerie will check whether you're real.
YOUTUBE — CONVERSION
Not yours. Theirs.
Don't build a channel; you'd be filming for a year. Buy nothing — trade lifetime unlock codes to two mid-size collector channels and let them catalogue their own shelf on camera. Their fifteen-minute video converts better than anything you'd post, because the shelf is theirs and the doubt is real. Shorts you can do yourself as clip repurposing, zero extra work.

Post the same clip to all three, vertical 9:16, but re-cut the first two seconds per platform — that's where the algorithm decides. Everything after second three can be identical.

02 · Before you film
What AI can and
can't make here

Generative video cannot render your interface. It will invent a plausible-looking app that isn't yours, with text that melts — and for a product whose whole argument is craft, a fake UI is the worst possible first impression. So split every clip in two:

AI GENERATES
The world around the object
Crates in an attic, a flea market at dawn, dust in light, hands lifting a sleeve, a shelf in a warm room. Atmosphere, b-roll, transitions. Never the phone screen, never text, never a logo.
YOU SCREEN-RECORD
Every frame containing the app
The real prototype, on a real device, at 60fps. The sleeve turning, the sparkle on a special edition, the shelf scrolling, the history tab. This is the payoff shot in every single clip below and it must be genuine.

Each prompt below therefore has two parts: a generation prompt you can paste into a video model for the b-roll, and a capture list of what to record from the app. Copy is written to be spoken flatly, without enthusiasm — collectors distrust enthusiasm.

03 · Eight clips
The prompts
01
The inherited crate
TIKTOK
24s
HIGHEST CEILING
FIRST TWO SECONDS — SPOKEN, NO MUSIC YET
"My dad left me four hundred records and I don't know what a single one of them is."
GENERATION PROMPT — B-ROLL
Handheld vertical video, 9:16. A dim garage in late afternoon, one small window, dust suspended in a shaft of light. Three cardboard boxes crammed with vinyl records on a concrete floor. A pair of adult hands tips a box forward and fans the spines apart; the sleeves are worn, corners soft. Natural light only, slight camera shake, shallow depth of field, warm faded colour, 35mm film grain. No text, no screens, no logos, no faces.
CAPTURE LIST — REAL APP
0–6s
AI b-roll of the crate, hook spoken over it.
6–15s
Screen record: camera in photo mode, shoot a crate spine-on, results filling the review queue. Real-time, no speed ramp — the speed is the point.
15–21s
Screen record: collection list scrolling, then one object opening and the sleeve turning.
21–24s
Cut back to the real crate, now empty. Text on screen: "400 records. One evening."
Why this one first: inheritance is the story every collector app ignores, and it's the only hook that reaches non-collectors — audience C from the brief. Comments will be people describing their own unopened boxes.

04 · Running it
Rules and cadence
RULE 1
Never open on the logo
Open on the object or the problem. The app appears at second five, once someone has a reason to care.
RULE 2
Real speed, no speed ramps
The claim is "ten seconds per object". Speeding up the footage secretly admits it isn't true, and collectors notice.
RULE 3
Text on screen, always
Most views are muted. Every hook and every payoff line exists as burned-in text as well as voice.
RULE 4
One hobby per clip
A clip about "collections" reaches nobody. A clip about vinyl reaches vinyl people, who are the only ones who'll comment — and comments are the distribution.
RULE 5
Answer every comment for the first month
Especially the hostile ones about grading. It's free research, and it's how you find Founding Cataloguers.
RULE 6
No paid spend until one clip clears 50k organically
That's your proof of a working creative. Then put €300 behind that one clip, measure cost per payer, not per install, and stop the moment it exceeds €21.
A realistic cadence for one person

Two clips a week, both filmed in the same session: one sleeve-turn loop (ten minutes) and one story clip from the eight above (two hours including the b-roll generation). Post each to TikTok and Reels with a re-cut opening; drop the loops into Shorts as filler. That's eight clips a month, ninety a year, and one of them working is the entire plan — this is a lottery where the tickets are cheap and you print them yourself.
